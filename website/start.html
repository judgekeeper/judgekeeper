Use it on your app

One command, in your project folder. It finds what your eval tool already saved, you mark answers Correct or Wrong in your browser, and it shows how often your judge agrees with you.

First install judgekeeper inside your project, if you have not yet. Then, in the folder of your AI app, run:

cd your-project
judgekeeper start

What you see, for a project with promptfoo results:

Looking in /Users/me/support-bot ...

✓ Your eval tool: promptfoo (results.json, saved 2026-10-03 14:12)
✓ Your judge: llm-rubric "is polite and correct" with openai:gpt-4.1-mini
✓ 212 answers with a verdict: the judge passed 171 and failed 41

You will label answers in your browser, one at a time: Correct or Wrong.
You won't see what the judge said. judgekeeper picks half from the judge's
passes and half from its fails, so you see enough of both.

  A rough check needs 15 Correct and 15 Wrong.
  A reliable result needs 25 Correct and 25 Wrong.
  Most people need 10 to 20 minutes.

Open the labeling page now? [Y/n]

Each line starts with what it found. Press Enter to open the labeling page.

What it reads

The results your eval tool already saved in your project folder. It looks for:

  • promptfoo: the results file that promptfoo eval -o or promptfoo export eval writes.
  • DeepEval: the test runs DeepEval saves, in .deepeval/ or the folder DEEPEVAL_RESULTS_FOLDER names.
  • Inspect AI: the logs in logs/.
  • MLflow: the assessments in a local mlruns/ folder or mlflow.db.
  • A table you made: a CSV or JSONL file with an input, an output and a verdict column.
  • Your judge's own results file, after judgekeeper setup: it reads judgekeeper.toml first (below).
  • judgekeeper.record(): the verdicts it saved in .judgekeeper/records/ (below).

It names your judge from the results: its name, the first line of its rubric and its model. Several tools or several judges? It asks which one. Too few answers? It says how many there are and prints the command that makes more with your own tool. You run that command, not judgekeeper. Your judge saves its results in a format of its own? See below.

What it never does

  • No AI calls unless you say yes. Then your own judge runs through your own tool; judgekeeper never sees your key, it only checks its name.
  • It never runs your app. Only when you ask your judge again, and say yes, does it run your eval tool, to grade answers it already saved.
  • It never reads your keys. To tell you which key your judge will use, it looks at the names of the variables in .env files, never their values. It never looks outside your project folder.
  • It never reads promptfoo's own database. If promptfoo kept the results there, judgekeeper offers to run promptfoo export for you, which writes them to a file. It asks first.
  • It never changes your .gitignore.

The labeling page

The page runs on your own computer. It shows one answer at a time: the question your app got and the answer it wrote. You never see what the judge said, so it cannot sway you.

Press 1 for Correct (this answer should pass) and 2 for Wrong (it should fail). S skips an answer you cannot judge from the text, and U undoes the last one. Every click is saved at once, so you can stop at any time.

The counts at the top are your own: how many you marked Correct and how many Wrong. A rough check needs 15 of each, a reliable result 25 of each. judgekeeper shows half answers the judge passed and half it failed, so you see enough of both.

The result

Press See my result at any time, or label every answer. The result shows in the page and in the terminal, and it is saved as .judgekeeper/result.html, which opens without judgekeeper running.

It starts with two plain sentences: of the answers you marked Correct, how many your judge passed, and of the answers you marked Wrong, how many it failed. Each comes with the range it is likely to lie in. Then one line says whether it agrees with you often enough to use as a gate. A gate is an automatic check that can stop a release.

  • TPR: of the answers you marked Correct, the share the judge passed.
  • TNR: of the answers you marked Wrong, the share the judge failed.
  • Kappa: how much the judge agrees with you beyond lucky guessing.

Because the page showed far more of the judge's fails than your results hold, the numbers are corrected for that picking, so they describe all of your answers.

Review the disagreements

Free, with no AI call. A disagreement is an answer where your label and your judge's verdict differ.

  1. Look again. You see each disagreement again, mixed with as many answers you and the judge agreed on, and the judge's verdict is still hidden. Mark each one Correct, Wrong or Not sure.
  2. See what your judge said. For each disagreement you see the judge's verdict and its reason, and choose: The judge was wrong, I slipped or The rule is unclear.

The judge's mistakes go to .judgekeeper/judge-mistakes.csv: the answers to improve your judge's rule with. Unclear cases go to rule-unclear.csv. Your first labels stay the main result.

judgekeeper start --review

Ask your judge again

judgekeeper has your own eval tool grade the answers you labeled again, with your own judge. Your app is not run. It works with promptfoo, DeepEval, Inspect AI and MLflow, or with your own judge as a command (--judge-command).

Before any call it shows a plan: your judge, and whether this is exactly your judge, a close copy (and what differs), or a judge it can't ask again (and why); which key your judge will use, by name only; how many judge calls, and a cost range at prices dated in judgekeeper; and what the run touches. Then it asks Go ahead? [y/N]. The answer is No unless you type y.

Then it shows how often your judge changed its verdict on the same answer, how well it agrees with you today next to your last result, and how often its new verdict matches the one it saved.

judgekeeper start --ask-again

Try your new judge

You fixed your judge's rule or changed its model, and ran your eval once. judgekeeper start notices the new judge and offers to try it on the answers you already marked. You mark nothing new for this.

It rebuilds the new judge from your newest results and has it grade your marked answers, never your new outputs. It shows the same plan and asks the same question as asking again. Then it shows your old judge and your new judge side by side, against the same marks.

A judge changed after you saw its mistakes on these answers will look better on them. So judgekeeper then offers a quick check: you mark 10 Correct and 10 Wrong answers from your newest results that you never marked. Its result shows the wide ranges that come with so few answers.

Your main result stays your old judge's. To check the new judge from now on, run judgekeeper start --new: everything moves to .judgekeeper/previous-<date>/, and nothing is deleted.

judgekeeper start --try-new-judge

Running it again

Your labels and results are saved in .judgekeeper/ in your project folder. It holds your answers' text, so commit it only if your data may live in your repository. Run judgekeeper start again and it carries on:

  • You stopped before the end: it asks Continue? and opens the page at the next answer.
  • You have a result: it asks what next: review the answers where you and your judge disagree (free), ask your judge again, Label more, or nothing for now.
  • Your judge changed: when your newest results come from a new version of your judge, it also offers to try the new judge on the answers you already marked.
  • You ran your eval again: it does a re-check. It finds the answers you labeled in the new results, says whether your judge changed, and shows the result next to the last one. If your app now writes different answers, your old labels no longer fit, and it offers to label the new results.
  • You want to start over: judgekeeper start --new moves everything to .judgekeeper/previous-<date>/. Nothing is deleted.

In a script, CI or a coding assistant, it never asks: it prints the flag that answers the question. Labeling needs a person. Every flag is in the reference.

Your judge saves results its own way

Many judges are code you wrote, and they save their results in a format of their own. Then judgekeeper start finds nothing it can read. There are three ways in. Pick one.

1. judgekeeper setup: no change to your code

In your project folder, run:

judgekeeper setup

It finds a recent JSON, JSONL or CSV file that seems to hold your judge's results, nested or flat, lists what one line holds, and says how it will read it: what one answer is, the input, the answer, the score or verdict and the reason. It asks only what it cannot tell, for example whether two answers per case (A and B) each count as an answer, and which judge to check. It shows 3 answers as it reads them. Then it asks once before it changes any file: it saves where your results are and how to read them in judgekeeper.toml, adds .judgekeeper/ to .gitignore and judgekeeper to your dev requirements. It never edits your code. Then run judgekeeper start. Your results are somewhere else? judgekeeper setup path/to/results.jsonl.

2. One judgekeeper.record() line

Your judge runs in your own code? Add one line right after it gives each verdict, in your eval code. Then run your eval once, and judgekeeper start finds the verdicts.

import judgekeeper
judgekeeper.record(input=question, output=answer, score=0.82, pass_mark=0.5,
                   reason=reason, judge="claude-opus-5", rule=criteria, name="Safe wording")

It never stops your program, uses only Python's standard library, and JUDGEKEEPER_RECORD=0 turns it off. Without the import, or in TypeScript: judgekeeper record --snippet python (or typescript) prints a few lines that do the same. judgekeeper never edits your code, but your coding agent can add the line. Paste this prompt: it shows you the change and waits for your yes.

In this project, my LLM judge runs in my own code. Please add the judgekeeper.record() line,
so that judgekeeper can check my judge against my own labels:

1. judgekeeper must be installed in this project's own Python environment, as a dev tool. If
   it is missing, install it there: for example `uv add --dev judgekeeper`, `poetry add
   --group dev judgekeeper`, or `pip install judgekeeper` with the project's virtual
   environment active.
2. Find where this project's LLM judge produces each score or verdict.
3. Right after it, add one call, with the values the judge just used and gave:

       import judgekeeper
       judgekeeper.record(input=..., output=..., score=..., pass_mark=..., reason=...,
                          judge="<the judge's model>", rule=<the judge's rule or criteria>,
                          name="<the judge's or criterion's name>")

   When the judge gives pass or fail, pass verdict=... instead of score and pass_mark. One
   call per judge or criterion. Only in the eval code, never in code that serves real users
   (it saves inputs and outputs to a file); JUDGEKEEPER_RECORD=0 turns it off.
4. Touch nothing else. record() catches its own errors and returns nothing, so it can never
   change what the program does; do not wrap it in code that could.
5. Show me the diff and wait for my yes before saving.
6. Then run the eval once, run `judgekeeper import records .judgekeeper/records --check`, and
   show me what it prints.

judgekeeper record --agent-prompt prints the same prompt in your terminal.

3. Your coding agent writes a table

Turn your results into a table: a CSV with the columns id, input, output, verdict (pass or fail) and reason, plus judge_model when you know which model judged. Make one CSV per judge or criterion. Then run:

judgekeeper start your-table.csv

When it reads a table, it prints how many answers it found, how many the judge passed and failed, and the first 3 rows, so a wrong table shows at once.

Your coding agent (Claude Code, Cursor or Codex) can write that table for you. Paste this prompt:

In this project, my LLM judge saves its results in its own format. Please:

1. Install judgekeeper into this project's own Python environment, as a dev tool: for
   example `uv add --dev judgekeeper`, `poetry add --group dev judgekeeper`, or
   `pip install judgekeeper` with the project's virtual environment active.
2. Find where the judge's results are saved, and write a small script that turns them into
   judgekeeper's table: a CSV with the columns id, input, output, verdict and reason, plus
   judge_model when you can tell which model judged. The verdict is pass or fail.
3. Write one CSV per judge or criterion. Leave out rows where the judge gave an error, and
   scores made by a rule instead of an LLM.
4. Keep everything on this computer: never send the data anywhere.
5. Run `judgekeeper start <the CSV file>` and show me what it prints, so I can check the
   counts and the example rows.

judgekeeper start --agent-prompt prints the same prompt in your terminal.

The records format

judgekeeper.record() and the snippets write judgekeeper's own format: one JSON line per verdict. It has a version and keeps fields it does not know, so files you write today still read later. judgekeeper import records your-file.jsonl --check checks a file and lists every problem with its line. The fields are in the reference, and the rules as a JSON Schema.

Install judgekeeper

judgekeeper is a tool for developers, like pytest. Install it inside your project, in the project's own Python environment, not on your computer as a whole. Pick your computer. Linux follows the Mac tab. Under the steps is a fix for each thing that can go wrong.

  1. Check Python

    Open the Terminal app and run:

    python3 --version

    What you see. Python 3.11 or a higher number, such as Python 3.13.2. If you see command not found or a lower number, install Python from python.org/downloads and open a new terminal.

  2. Switch on your project's environment

    An environment (a virtual environment) is a folder in your project that holds its own Python and what the project needs. Go to your project and switch it on:

    cd your-project
source .venv/bin/activate

    What you see. The start of the line now reads (.venv). If your project keeps its environment in another folder, such as venv, use that name.

    No .venv folder in your project yet (no such file or directory)? Make one, then run the source line again:

    python3 -m venv .venv

  3. Install judgekeeper

    pip install judgekeeper

    What you see. A few lines. One near the end starts with Successfully installed. A notice about a newer pip may follow. You can ignore it.

    Does your project use uv or Poetry? Add judgekeeper as a dev tool instead, with one of these:

    uv add --dev judgekeeper
    poetry add --group dev judgekeeper

  4. Check it runs

    judgekeeper --version

    What you see. A line that ends in is ready, then what to run next. If it also says judgekeeper is installed outside a project, your project's environment was not switched on: go back to step 2.

If step 3 was refused: error: externally-managed-environment

Your project's environment is not switched on, so pip tried to install into the Python of your whole computer, which Homebrew or the system protects. It is not about judgekeeper. Run step 2, check that the line starts with (.venv), then install again.

pip: command not found

A Mac may print it as zsh: command not found: pip. Outside an environment, a Mac names the command pip3. Switch on your project's environment (step 2): inside it, pip works.

The judgekeeper command is not found

With your project's environment switched on, run python -m judgekeeper --version. It does the same thing as judgekeeper --version. Put python -m in front of every judgekeeper command. With uv or Poetry, put uv run or poetry run in front instead.

  1. Check Python

    Open PowerShell and run:

    py --version

    What you see. Python 3.11 or a higher number, such as Python 3.13.2. When Python is on the PATH, python --version works too. If you see 'py' is not recognized or a lower number, install Python from python.org/downloads and open a new PowerShell window.

  2. Switch on your project's environment

    An environment (a virtual environment) is a folder in your project that holds its own Python and what the project needs. Go to your project and switch it on:

    cd your-project
.venv\Scripts\activate

    What you see. The start of the line now reads (.venv). If your project keeps its environment in another folder, such as venv, use that name.

    No .venv folder in your project yet (is not recognized or cannot find the path)? Make one, then run the activate line again:

    py -m venv .venv

    If PowerShell refuses to run the script, run Set-ExecutionPolicy -ExecutionPolicy RemoteSigned -Scope CurrentUser once and try again. In Command Prompt the line is .venv\Scripts\activate.bat.

  3. Install judgekeeper

    pip install judgekeeper

    What you see. A few lines. One near the end starts with Successfully installed. A notice about a newer pip may follow. You can ignore it.

    Does your project use uv or Poetry? Add judgekeeper as a dev tool instead, with one of these:

    uv add --dev judgekeeper
    poetry add --group dev judgekeeper

  4. Check it runs

    judgekeeper --version

    What you see. A line that ends in is ready, then what to run next. If it also says judgekeeper is installed outside a project, your project's environment was not switched on: go back to step 2.

If step 3 was refused: error: externally-managed-environment

Your project's environment is not switched on, so pip tried to install into the Python of your whole computer, which another program or the system protects. It is not about judgekeeper. Run step 2, check that the line starts with (.venv), then install again.

'pip' is not recognized as an internal or external command

PowerShell words it as The term 'pip' is not recognized. Inside your project's environment pip is always found, so the environment is not switched on: run step 2 again. Or use the environment's own Python, which always installs into your project:

.venv\Scripts\python -m pip install judgekeeper

What you see. The same as in step 3. Go on to step 4.

The judgekeeper command is not found

With your project's environment switched on, run python -m judgekeeper --version. It does the same thing as judgekeeper --version. Put python -m in front of every judgekeeper command. With uv or Poetry, put uv run or poetry run in front instead.

Add judgekeeper to your project's dev requirements, so everyone who works on the project gets it. The package is judgekeeper on PyPI, and the source code is on GitHub.

Other ways

No saved results, or you would rather work from a spreadsheet? These four steps check a judge with a table you make yourself.

  1. Collect answers from your app

    Save 30 to 60 real answers in one file named items.csv. Each row is one answer: an id, the input your app got and the output it wrote. Take ordinary cases and hard ones, good answers and bad ones. That many is enough for a first look. About 100 gives a firmer result.

    This step has no command. You make the file in a spreadsheet. From Excel, save it as CSV UTF-8.

    What the file looks like:

    id,input,output
q1,What is the capital of France?,Paris is the capital of France.
q2,What is the capital of Japan?,The capital of Japan is Tokyo.

  2. Label them

    You read each answer and say pass or fail. That mark is a human label. It is the one part no AI model may do for you: the labels are what the judge is checked against.

    judgekeeper label items.csv --out labels.csv

    What you see. A page opens in your browser. It runs on your own computer, and nothing leaves it. It shows one answer at a time. Press 1 for pass and 2 for fail. Every label is saved at once into labels.csv, so you can stop and come back. Press Ctrl-C in the terminal when you are done.

  3. Run your judge, or bring its verdicts

    A verdict is the judge's pass or fail on one answer. judgekeeper needs the judge's verdicts on the same answers you labeled.

    If your tools already saved the verdicts, put them next to your labels in one table. Open labels.csv in a spreadsheet, add a verdict column with what the judge said, rename human_label to label and save it as results.csv. Then run:

    judgekeeper check results.csv --judge verdict --human label

    What you see. This is the result for the small table in the tutorial:

    Of the answers people passed, the judge passed 100%. Of the answers people failed, the judge failed 33%. It is not trustworthy as a gate.
Not trustworthy as a gate: TPR 1.00, TNR 0.33, kappa 0.41 against human labels.
  - TNR is 0.33 (below 0.80): not trustworthy as a gate.
  - Kappa vs human labels is 0.41 (below 0.6): not trustworthy as a gate.
  - judge identity incomplete: provider, model, snapshot, endpoint, prompt_hash, rubric_version, temperature
  - Only 10 labeled items: error bars are wide; aim for about 100.
wrote judgekeeper-report/report.json and judgekeeper-report/report.html
    No saved verdicts? judgekeeper can run your judge.

    The first command freezes your labels so nobody can change them later. The second runs the judge three times. The third compares it with your labels and prints the same kind of result.

    judgekeeper import-labels labels.csv -o anchors.jsonl
judgekeeper judge anchors.jsonl --callable my_judges:judge --runs 3 --out runs/mine
judgekeeper validate anchors.jsonl runs/mine --out reports/mine

    Here my_judges:judge is your own Python function. For other kinds of judge, see Works with what you already use. The tutorial shows each command with its output.

  4. Read the result

    The first line is the result in plain words: how often the judge passed the answers you passed, how often it failed the answers you failed, and one of three verdicts: usable as a gate, usable with care or not trustworthy as a gate. A gate is an automatic check that can stop a release.

    The second line gives the same result as three numbers:

    • TPR: of the answers you passed, the share the judge also passed.
    • TNR: of the answers you failed, the share the judge also failed.
    • Kappa: how much the judge agrees with you beyond lucky guessing. 0 is no better than chance and 1 is perfect.

    The lines that start with a dash are flags: things to know before you rely on the judge. The last line names the full report. Open it in your browser:

    open judgekeeper-report/report.html

    What you see. One page with the same verdict, and every answer you and the judge disagree on, with the judge's own reason. On Windows, use start in place of open. On Linux, use xdg-open. See a real report.
