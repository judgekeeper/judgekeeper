Use it on your app

One command, in your project folder. It finds what your eval tool already saved, you mark answers Correct or Wrong in your browser, and it shows how often your judge agrees with you.

First install judgekeeper, if you have not yet. Then go to the folder of your AI app and run:

cd your-project
judgekeeper start

What you see, for a project with promptfoo results:

Looking in /Users/me/support-bot ...

✓ Your eval tool: promptfoo (results.json, saved 2026-10-03 14:12)
✓ Your judge: llm-rubric "is polite and correct" with openai:gpt-4.1-mini
✓ 212 answers with a verdict: the judge passed 171 and failed 41

You will label answers in your browser, one at a time: Correct or Wrong.
You won't see what the judge said. judgekeeper picks half from the judge's
passes and half from its fails, so you see enough of both.

  A rough check needs 15 Correct and 15 Wrong.
  A reliable result needs 25 Correct and 25 Wrong.
  Most people need 10 to 20 minutes.

Open the labeling page now? [Y/n]

Each line starts with what it found. Press Enter to open the labeling page.

What it reads

The results your eval tool already saved in your project folder. It looks for:

  • promptfoo: the results file that promptfoo eval -o or promptfoo export eval writes.
  • DeepEval: the test runs DeepEval saves, in .deepeval/ or the folder DEEPEVAL_RESULTS_FOLDER names.
  • Inspect AI: the logs in logs/.
  • MLflow: the assessments in a local mlruns/ folder or mlflow.db.
  • A table you made: a CSV or JSONL file with an input, an output and a verdict column.

It names your judge from the results: its name, the first line of its rubric and its model. Several tools or several judges? It asks which one. Too few answers? It says how many there are and prints the command that makes more with your own tool. You run that command, not judgekeeper.

What it never does

  • No AI calls, and no API key. Everything comes from files your tools already saved.
  • It never runs your app, your eval or your code.
  • It never reads .env files or your keys, and it never looks outside your project folder.
  • It never reads promptfoo's own database. If promptfoo kept the results there, judgekeeper prints the promptfoo export command that writes them to a file, and you run it.
  • It never changes your .gitignore.

The labeling page

The page runs on your own computer. It shows one answer at a time: the question your app got and the answer it wrote. You never see what the judge said, so it cannot sway you.

Press 1 for Correct (this answer should pass) and 2 for Wrong (it should fail). S skips an answer you cannot judge from the text, and U undoes the last one. Every click is saved at once, so you can stop at any time.

The counts at the top are your own: how many you marked Correct and how many Wrong. A rough check needs 15 of each, a reliable result 25 of each. judgekeeper shows half answers the judge passed and half it failed, so you see enough of both.

The result

Press See my result at any time, or label every answer. The result shows in the page and in the terminal, and it is saved as .judgekeeper/result.html, which opens without judgekeeper running.

It starts with two plain sentences: of the answers you marked Correct, how many your judge passed, and of the answers you marked Wrong, how many it failed. Each comes with the range it is likely to lie in. Then one line says whether it agrees with you often enough to use as a gate. A gate is an automatic check that can stop a release.

  • TPR: of the answers you marked Correct, the share the judge passed.
  • TNR: of the answers you marked Wrong, the share the judge failed.
  • Kappa: how much the judge agrees with you beyond lucky guessing.

Because the page showed far more of the judge's fails than your results hold, the numbers are corrected for that picking, so they describe all of your answers.

Running it again

Your labels and results are saved in .judgekeeper/ in your project folder. It holds your answers' text, so commit it only if your data may live in your repository. Run judgekeeper start again and it carries on:

  • You stopped before the end: it asks Continue? and opens the page at the next answer.
  • You have a result: it asks what next: review the answers where you and your judge disagree (free), Label more, or nothing for now.
  • You ran your eval again: it does a re-check. It finds the answers you labeled in the new results, says whether your judge changed, and shows the result next to the last one. If your app now writes different answers, your old labels no longer fit, and it offers to label the new results.
  • You want to start over: judgekeeper start --new moves everything to .judgekeeper/previous-<date>/. Nothing is deleted.

In a script, CI or a coding assistant, it never asks: it prints the flag that answers the question. Labeling needs a person. Every flag is in the reference.

Install judgekeeper

Three steps. Pick your computer. Linux follows the Mac tab. Under the steps is a fix for each thing that can go wrong.

  1. Check Python

    Open the Terminal app and run:

    python3 --version

    What you see. Python 3.11 or a higher number, such as Python 3.13.2. If you see command not found or a lower number, install Python from python.org/downloads and open a new terminal.

  2. Install judgekeeper

    pip3 install judgekeeper

    What you see. A few lines. One near the end starts with Successfully installed. A notice about a newer pip may follow. You can ignore it.

  3. Check it runs

    judgekeeper --version

    What you see. One line: judgekeeper and the version number.

If step 2 was refused: error: externally-managed-environment

This Python is managed by Homebrew or by the system, and it refuses direct installs to protect itself. It is not about judgekeeper. The fix is a virtual environment: a folder that holds a Python program and what it needs, kept apart from the rest of your computer. Make one:

python3 -m venv ~/judgekeeper-env

What you see. Nothing. It takes a few seconds. Now switch it on:

source ~/judgekeeper-env/bin/activate

What you see. The start of the line now reads (judgekeeper-env). Now install:

pip install judgekeeper

What you see. A few lines. One near the end starts with Successfully installed. Go on to step 3.

Each time you open a new terminal, run the source line again before you use judgekeeper.

pip: command not found

A Mac may print it as zsh: command not found: pip. On a Mac the command is named pip3. Use the line in step 2 as it is written.

The judgekeeper command is not found

Run python3 -m judgekeeper --version. It does the same thing as judgekeeper --version. Put python3 -m in front of every judgekeeper command.

  1. Check Python

    Open PowerShell and run:

    py --version

    What you see. Python 3.11 or a higher number, such as Python 3.13.2. When Python is on the PATH, python --version works too. If you see 'py' is not recognized or a lower number, install Python from python.org/downloads and open a new PowerShell window.

  2. Install judgekeeper

    pip install judgekeeper

    What you see. A few lines. One near the end starts with Successfully installed. A notice about a newer pip may follow. You can ignore it.

  3. Check it runs

    judgekeeper --version

    What you see. One line: judgekeeper and the version number.

If step 2 was refused: error: externally-managed-environment

This Python is managed by another program or by the system, and it refuses direct installs to protect itself. It is not about judgekeeper. The fix is a virtual environment: a folder that holds a Python program and what it needs, kept apart from the rest of your computer. Make one:

py -m venv "$HOME\judgekeeper-env"

What you see. Nothing. It takes a few seconds. Now switch it on:

& "$HOME\judgekeeper-env\Scripts\Activate.ps1"

What you see. The start of the line now reads (judgekeeper-env). Now install:

pip install judgekeeper

What you see. A few lines. One near the end starts with Successfully installed. Go on to step 3.

Each time you open a new PowerShell window, run the Activate.ps1 line again before you use judgekeeper.

In Command Prompt, the two lines are py -m venv "%USERPROFILE%\judgekeeper-env" and "%USERPROFILE%\judgekeeper-env\Scripts\activate.bat". If PowerShell refuses to run the script, run Set-ExecutionPolicy -ExecutionPolicy RemoteSigned -Scope CurrentUser once and try again.

'pip' is not recognized as an internal or external command

PowerShell words it as The term 'pip' is not recognized. Python was installed without being added to the PATH, the list of places Windows looks for commands. The py command is installed either way, so use it:

py -m pip install judgekeeper

What you see. The same as in step 2. Go on to step 3.

The judgekeeper command is not found

Run py -m judgekeeper --version. It does the same thing as judgekeeper --version. Put py -m in front of every judgekeeper command.

Already use uv? uvx judgekeeper --version runs it without installing. The package is judgekeeper on PyPI, and the source code is on GitHub.

Other ways

No saved results, or you would rather work from a spreadsheet? These four steps check a judge with a table you make yourself.

  1. Collect answers from your app

    Save 30 to 60 real answers in one file named items.csv. Each row is one answer: an id, the input your app got and the output it wrote. Take ordinary cases and hard ones, good answers and bad ones. That many is enough for a first look. About 100 gives a firmer result.

    This step has no command. You make the file in a spreadsheet. From Excel, save it as CSV UTF-8.

    What the file looks like:

    id,input,output
q1,What is the capital of France?,Paris is the capital of France.
q2,What is the capital of Japan?,The capital of Japan is Tokyo.

  2. Label them

    You read each answer and say pass or fail. That mark is a human label. It is the one part no AI model may do for you: the labels are what the judge is checked against.

    judgekeeper label items.csv --out labels.csv

    What you see. A page opens in your browser. It runs on your own computer, and nothing leaves it. It shows one answer at a time. Press 1 for pass and 2 for fail. Every label is saved at once into labels.csv, so you can stop and come back. Press Ctrl-C in the terminal when you are done.

  3. Run your judge, or bring its verdicts

    A verdict is the judge's pass or fail on one answer. judgekeeper needs the judge's verdicts on the same answers you labeled.

    If your tools already saved the verdicts, put them next to your labels in one table. Open labels.csv in a spreadsheet, add a verdict column with what the judge said, rename human_label to label and save it as results.csv. Then run:

    judgekeeper check results.csv --judge verdict --human label

    What you see. This is the result for the small table in the tutorial:

    Of the answers people passed, the judge passed 100%. Of the answers people failed, the judge failed 33%. It is not trustworthy as a gate.
Not trustworthy as a gate: TPR 1.00, TNR 0.33, kappa 0.41 against human labels.
  - TNR is 0.33 (below 0.80): not trustworthy as a gate.
  - Kappa vs human labels is 0.41 (below 0.6): not trustworthy as a gate.
  - judge identity incomplete: provider, model, snapshot, endpoint, prompt_hash, rubric_version, temperature
  - Only 10 labeled items: error bars are wide; aim for about 100.
wrote judgekeeper-report/report.json and judgekeeper-report/report.html
    No saved verdicts? judgekeeper can run your judge.

    The first command freezes your labels so nobody can change them later. The second runs the judge three times. The third compares it with your labels and prints the same kind of result.

    judgekeeper import-labels labels.csv -o anchors.jsonl
judgekeeper judge anchors.jsonl --callable my_judges:judge --runs 3 --out runs/mine
judgekeeper validate anchors.jsonl runs/mine --out reports/mine

    Here my_judges:judge is your own Python function. For other kinds of judge, see Works with what you already use. The tutorial shows each command with its output.

  4. Read the result

    The first line is the result in plain words: how often the judge passed the answers you passed, how often it failed the answers you failed, and one of three verdicts: usable as a gate, usable with care or not trustworthy as a gate. A gate is an automatic check that can stop a release.

    The second line gives the same result as three numbers:

    • TPR: of the answers you passed, the share the judge also passed.
    • TNR: of the answers you failed, the share the judge also failed.
    • Kappa: how much the judge agrees with you beyond lucky guessing. 0 is no better than chance and 1 is perfect.

    The lines that start with a dash are flags: things to know before you rely on the judge. The last line names the full report. Open it in your browser:

    open judgekeeper-report/report.html

    What you see. One page with the same verdict, and every answer you and the judge disagree on, with the judge's own reason. On Windows, use start in place of open. On Linux, use xdg-open. See a real report.
