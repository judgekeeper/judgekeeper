The problem

Why check your judge

Your AI product writes answers. To know whether they are good, you could read them all yourself, but that is slow. So teams ask another AI model to grade them: the judge. A judge is fast and cheap. It can also be wrong, in three ways.

It disagrees with people

A judge can pass an answer a person would fail. A judge that passes everything makes every test look fine.

It is inconsistent

Ask a judge the same question three times and you can get two different answers.

It changes silently

The company behind the judge model can update it. Then every score moves, although your product did not change.

The report's verdict uses these rules: not trustworthy as a gate if TPR or TNR is below 0.80 or kappa is below 0.6; usable with care if TPR or TNR is below 0.90; otherwise usable as a gate. A gate is an automatic check that can stop a release.

Integrations

Reads what your tools already save

judgekeeper does not replace your tools. It reads what they already save: the results of promptfoo, DeepEval, Inspect AI and MLflow, or a CSV or JSONL table. Run judgekeeper start in your project; the Guide shows each step.

Numbers

Do the ranges hold on real data?

Each number in a result comes with a range. In public datasets where a judge and people both decided on the same answers, we pretended a person marked only a few of them, and counted how often the range held the value worked out from all of the dataset's human labels. No judge was called.

  • On real data. On 5 real judges from 3 public datasets (LLMBar, MT-Bench and LLMJudge), with 50 human labels per check, the ranges for how often the judge passed answers people passed (TPR) and how often it failed answers people failed (TNR) held that value in 96.0 checks in 100 on average (lowest 94.6, highest 98.4), over 1,000 checks per judge.
  • One of those checks. GPT-4 as the judge on MT-Bench, 50 labels: TPR 0.82 (range 0.71 to 0.91), and the value from all 1,814 human labels is 0.85; TNR 0.49 (range 0.36 to 0.65), and from all labels 0.56.
  • In simulation too. The ranges held the true value at least 93.6 times in 100 in every tested case, and 96.2 on average.
  • Tested on Python 3.11 to 3.14 on Linux, and on Windows.

Privacy

Your data and keys stay with you

An API key is the password your code uses to call a model provider. With judgekeeper, your key goes from your machine straight to your provider, and nowhere else.

  • No server. judgekeeper runs where you run it. Nothing is sent to its makers.
  • No key flag. No option takes a key.
  • Keys come only from environment variables, settings your shell or CI passes to programs, such as ANTHROPIC_API_KEY.
  • A test checks it. judgekeeper's own test suite checks that no key appears in any file or message it writes.
  • Error messages are scrubbed. Anything that looks like a key is replaced with [REDACTED] before it is printed.

FAQ

Questions

How many answers do I need to mark?

judgekeeper shows a result once you have marked enough of each; marking more narrows the ranges.

Why TPR and TNR first, and not kappa?

They are easier to act on. TNR in particular catches a judge that passes everything, which raw agreement hides. Kappa is in the report too, next to them.

Does it cost anything?

judgekeeper is free. The only cost is your judge's own API calls, which you pay to your model provider. Reading your saved results and marking answers call no model.

Does it replace my eval framework?

No. It works alongside promptfoo, DeepEval, Inspect AI, MLflow or your own code, and reads the files they already save.

Does my data leave my machine?

No. judgekeeper has no server. The only network calls are the ones your judge makes to your model provider, and reads from your MLflow server when your results live there.
