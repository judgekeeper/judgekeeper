How it works

What the numbers mean, why some marks are kept aside, and how a re-check works.

Why check an LLM-as-a-judge

An LLM-as-a-judge grades answers Pass or Fail with a rule you wrote. Your eval scores are only as good as those grades. A judge can pass answers you would fail, fail answers you would pass, or change when you switch models.

To know, compare it with a person. You mark a few dozen answers once, and judgekeeper shows how often your judge agrees with you.

The first check

Before you mark anything, judgekeeper looks at your judge's grades for signs it isn't really judging. No AI call.

  • The same grade on every answer.
  • Errors or empty replies counted as Pass.
  • Answers with no clear Pass or Fail.

If it finds one, it says so, for example: Your judge passed every answer. It may not be checking anything.

What the numbers mean

judgekeeper measures two things, separately:

  • When you say Pass, how often your judge also says Pass.
  • When you say Fail, how often your judge also says Fail.

They are kept apart on purpose. A judge that passes almost everything looks great on the first and bad on the second. One combined score would hide that.

Why every number has a range

Flip a coin 10 times and get 7 heads: the coin doesn't land heads 70% of the time. With few flips, the result moves a lot. Your marks are the same: a sample of your answers. So each number is an estimate, and the range shows where the real number could be.

ResultWhen

Marks kept aside

A rule written from your judge's mistakes will do well on those mistakes. That says little about other answers. So judgekeeper keeps some of your marks aside, like exam questions you have not seen, and tests a new rule only on those.

It asks your judge about each of them with the old rule and the new one, then counts:

  • Fixed: the old rule got it wrong, the new rule gets it right.
  • Broke: the old rule got it right, the new rule gets it wrong.

Examples. Your result always comes from your own marks.

Each new rule you test on the same marks has to clear a higher bar, so trying many rules can't win a "Better" by coincidence. judgekeeper never changes your files: you copy a rule you like into your config yourself.

How a re-check works

Your marks belong to the answers, not to the judge. So after a change they are a fair test: the same exam, before and after. judgekeeper check compares the old and the new judge on every answer you marked, and counts which went from right to wrong and which from wrong to right.

It notices when you change:

WhatExample
The modelgpt-4.1-mini → gpt-4.1-nano
The ruleYou edited the rule text
The pass markA score of 0.5 → 0.7 counts as Pass
The temperatureHow random the model's replies are
The endpointA different server for the same model
Your eval tool's versionpromptfoo 0.124 → 0.125

If your new results already hold the new judge's grades, it needs no AI call. Otherwise it asks your new judge about each marked answer twice, to see how much the judge changes by itself. A change that small is not called a drop.

The result is Worse, No drop bigger than 20 points or Not sure.

Questions

Does my data leave my computer?

No. Your answers, marks and results stay in the .judgekeeper folder in your project. An AI call happens only when you say yes, through your own eval tool.

How many answers do I need to mark?

A few dozen, with enough Pass and enough Fail marks. "See your result" appears on the marking page when you have enough. More marks make the ranges smaller.

Which judges does it work for?

Any judge your eval tool runs, including open-source models on your own computer. judgekeeper reads the grades; it doesn't matter which model made them.

Can I switch eval tools and keep my marks?

Not in 1.0. judgekeeper says so and tells you how to start fresh.

Does it check my app?

No. It checks the LLM-as-a-judge, not whatever made the answers.
